QUESTION 1: What is AI governance in higher education?
====================================================================================================================
AI governance in higher education refers to the responsibility of senior management to formulate policies, establish ethical guidelines, and provide overall institutional direction for AI integration (Chan, 2024). This dimension focuses on ensuring that institutions operate within legal and ethical boundaries while striving for academic excellence and integrity (Chan, 2024). Senior management plays a crucial role in understanding, identifying, and preventing academic misconduct and ethical dilemmas related to AI use (Chan, 2024).

Effective governance requires addressing specific areas such as data privacy, transparency, accountability, and security (Chan, 2024). It involves establishing clear guidelines for the proper attribution of AI-generated content to promote academic honesty and clarity (Chan, 2024). Governance also necessitates implementing measures to ensure equitable access to AI technologies, thereby preventing the exacerbation of existing inequalities among students (Chan, 2024).

The ethical and sustainable development of artificial intelligence depends on good governance, which includes creating standards that are helpful to human society (Singh, 2024). Policymakers and institutions must develop data governance frameworks that protect student privacy and safeguard sensitive data through clear rules on collection and usage (PRESS, 2023). Without sound ethical standards and principles, the smooth implementation of AI in this sensitive sector is not possible (Singh, 2024).

Collaboration among all stakeholders, including universities, teachers, students, and staff, is essential for the successful integration of AI (Chan, 2024). A robust governance framework allows senior management to create a conducive environment for the ethical use of AI, mitigating risks associated with academic misconduct (Chan, 2024). Ultimately, these governance actions contribute to enhanced trust, compliance, and responsible AI usage within the academic community (Chan, 2024).

QUESTION 2: What evidence links virtual reality with student engagement in higher education?
====================================================================================================================
Research indicates that virtual reality (VR) contributes to increased learner engagement by providing interactive and experiential learning opportunities that require active participation ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). Unlike traditional teaching methods which often rely on passive learning, VR allows students to explore virtual worlds, perform simulations, and engage in scenarios that encourage critical thinking and decision-making ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). A study comparing VR-based learning environments to traditional classroom settings found a clear and statistically significant increase in student engagement levels among those using VR ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). This increase is attributed to the immersive nature of the technology, which fosters deeper involvement, higher motivation, and improved retention of knowledge ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.).

Qualitative data from students further supports these findings by highlighting themes such as increased motivation, enjoyment, and a heightened sense of presence within the learning material ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). Students reported feeling more motivated to participate due to the dynamic nature of VR sessions, which they found more enjoyable than traditional classroom activities ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). Other research confirms that immersive VR significantly outperforms desktop VR in terms of student motivation and sense of presence (Zaatar, 2024). Additionally, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled studies on anatomy education found that VR improved student interest in the subject matter (Zaatar, 2024).

Despite these positive links to engagement, several challenges affect the widespread adoption of VR in higher education. The high cost of equipment and software, along with ongoing maintenance expenses, presents a significant financial barrier for many institutions ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). Accessibility issues also arise, as not all students have equal access to the necessary technology due to financial constraints or lack of infrastructure ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). Furthermore, successful implementation requires instructors to possess specific technical skills and pedagogical training, which is often lacking ("The Impact of Virtual Reality", n.d.). Other studies identify potential health risks and difficulties in adapting to the technology as additional obstacles reported by students and faculty ("Information Technology Students' Perceptions Toward", 2024).

QUESTION 3: How is generative AI affecting academic integrity?
====================================================================================================================
Generative AI tools present significant challenges to academic integrity by enabling potential cheating and plagiarism through the generation of essays and assignments (AL-Smadi, 2022). The ease with which students can obtain detailed answers from these systems risks blurring the boundaries between independent research and reliance on AI-generated content (Chan, 2024). Consequently, 75% of students in a study of Ghanaian universities cited academic integrity as a primary concern when using Generative AI for content generation and research assistance ("Impact of Generative AI in", 2024).

To address these risks, scholars emphasize the necessity of developing clear ethical guidelines and implementing advanced plagiarism detection systems capable of identifying AI-generated text ("Impact of Generative AI in", 2024). Institutions are urged to establish specific policies that define misuse, such as flagging the use of AI to generate essay content as dishonest while allowing its use for data analysis with proper attribution (Chan, 2024). Furthermore, there is a strong call for robust governance frameworks that ensure transparency and accountability to maintain a culture of integrity within the academic community (Chan, 2024).

Educators also highlight the need for AI literacy programs to help students understand the capabilities and limitations of these tools, thereby promoting responsible use ("Impact of Generative AI in", 2024). A majority of faculty members consider training on ethical AI usage as highly important, with many advocating for assignments that foster critical thinking to mitigate overreliance ("Impact of Generative AI in", 2024). QUESTION 4: What evidence supports adaptive learning in higher education?
====================================================================================================================
Several studies suggest that adaptive learning can improve student outcomes in higher education by personalizing instruction based on individual needs and learning styles (Magaña, 2023). Research indicates that adaptive tutorials in mathematics higher education environments have led to significant improvements in subject knowledge confidence, with 98% of students reporting increased understanding (Weltman, 2018). Furthermore, data from these studies show that students who voluntarily used adaptive tutorials achieved higher median scores and a greater proportion of full marks compared to those who did not use them (Weltman, 2018).

The integration of artificial intelligence in STEM higher education is also cited as a key benefit for facilitating personalized learning experiences (PRESS, 2023). AI systems are described as capable of analyzing vast amounts of data on student performance and preferences to deliver tailored content, which helps optimize learning outcomes (PRESS, 2023). These systems can identify common misconceptions across groups of learners, allowing instructors to adjust their teaching strategies and curriculum design accordingly (PRESS, 2023).

However, the literature also highlights specific challenges associated with implementing these technologies in higher secondary and higher education contexts (Magaña, 2023). Reported obstacles include a lack of technology training for teachers, the costs involved, and the need for accurate and timely feedback mechanisms (Magaña, 2023). [partially supported] Additionally, ensuring the accuracy of algorithms, addressing data biases, and protecting student privacy are noted as critical ethical considerations for successful integration (PRESS, 2023).

QUESTION 5: What is Design Science Research Methodology and how is it used?
====================================================================================================================
Design Science Research (DSR) is described as an important and legitimate paradigm within Information Systems that involves the construction of socio-technical artifacts such as decision support systems, modeling tools, and governance strategies ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013). The paradigm has faced challenges regarding its full potential impact due to gaps in understanding and applying DSR concepts, leading to ongoing confusion about its central ideas and goals ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013). Within the field, DSR is sometimes referred to by alternative labels including "systemeering," a "constructive" approach, or an "engineering approach" ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013).

The methodology emphasizes that contributing to knowledge is the foremost criterion for publication, requiring researchers to address whether contributions are true, new, and interesting ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013). A key aspect of DSR is the development of artifacts where the design itself is a crucial element for revealing new knowledge that would not be attainable otherwise ("A systematic review of immersive", 2019). Researchers are encouraged to position their work within specific quadrants of a knowledge contribution framework, such as "Improvement" or "Exaptation," to clarify how their research advances the problem and solution domains ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013).

To communicate these contributions effectively, scholars propose a publication schema that substitutes the description of the DSR artifact for a traditional results section found in behavioral science papers ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013). [partially supported] This schema suggests that the introduction should specify the purpose and scope of the artifact, identify the class of problems it addresses, and clearly state the relevance to real-world practice ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013). The literature notes that while guidance exists on presenting DSR, there is often insufficient advice on how to establish contributions to generalized knowledge compared to behavioral science templates ("Positioning and Presenting Design Science", 2013).
